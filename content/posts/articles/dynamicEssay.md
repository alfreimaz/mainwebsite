## Introduction

The goal of this essay is to explore possible applications of the DEL
framework to one specific case: that of improvised theater. We know that
the relationship between knowledge and fiction has always been arduous,
or at the very least complicated ; the paradox of fiction being a great
example of their everlasting conflict. How can fictitious facts be known
if knowing requires truth? With only two ingredients already emerge
incredibly intricate questions. However, I personally believe that
improvisation, when added to the mix, results in even grander wonders.
Indeed, it implies one major key difference: the text has not yet been
written, and as such "truth\", even in its weakest definitions, appears
frail, unstable, undetermined. There is a fundamental lack of, well,
fundamentals, no baseline guiding the performance from start to end.
Improvisers decide what the story will be as they go. And although they
obviously must communicate it to the audience to make a compelling show,
thus avoiding entre-soi, they must also, even more crucially,
communicate it amongst themselves. This, more often than not, results in
misunderstandings and multiple interpretations of a same scene,
misunderstandings that the audience loves, as a peak behind the curtain
of a performer's mind, because the audience assumes what should have
happened. They "know\" the performer misremembered a character's name.
They "know\" that what has been set up will come back later in the
scene. And the improvisers "know\" where they want to take it.\
\
Could then the logical definition of knowledge, or at least certain
belief, be applied to such cases? What is the difference in treatment
between truths from the point of view of the actors and that of the
audience? Why are there statements that are easy to retract or bend for
great entertainment and some that, when reevaluated, make you wonder if
the people on stage are actually listening to one another? I will
attempt to give an answer, albeit a very partial one, to those
questions, using my knowledge and experience of improvised theater, both
on and off stage.

## A brief overview of improvised theater

Now, obviously, it would be hard to follow along without any idea of
what improvised theater is. I will be explaining the style which I am
most familiar with: the style most common to French-speaking
Switzerland, particularly Geneva [Fédération d'Improvisation Genevoise], though it has similarities with
that of Amsterdam.\
\
Improvised theater is, as anyone could guess, theater that is
improvised. In Geneva, it is played as a match, though this is of little
importance to us here. Most of the time, the scenes[^1] are quite short
and have to respect both a constraint of form and a theme. The
constraint can be any limitation or style imposed on the players (for
instance that all lines must rhyme, or that the scene must be played
following Western-movie codes). The theme on the other hand is generally
a single word or sentence to kickstart dialogue and plot-lines. Respect
of both is mandatory, under threat of a penalty for the unabiding team.
This necessarily means there is some meta aspect to improvisations; that
there are some elements that come from outside the dialogue and acting.
We will come back to this a few pages down.\
\
If you were to attend a show, you would notice that before a scene is
played out, both the constraint and its theme are announced by a
referee, then the players get a few seconds (called the "caucus\") to
argue for a general story, or to decide who plays which character.
Therefore, there is a small window of time to decide of what is, to
bastardise the term, common knowledge to all the players. However, not
all scenes allow for caucuses, as some constraints explicitly forbid
it.\
\
During improvisation, it is allowed for the actors not yet performing to
enter the stage at any time, and for any actor to leave and return to
the resting benches (again, if the constraint allows for it, as some
like "huis-clos\" explicitly anchor actors on stage). Thus, it is
possible for actors to scheme and discuss amongst benchwarmers before
they join in the scene. It is, however, not allowed for anyone outside
the scene to address anyone inside: once a player is on stage, all
exchange of information with them must go through acting, and acting
alone.\
\
Furthermore, all scenes are played under a time limit, imposed with the
constraint. Time is regularly communicated through hand signals given by
the referee, signals which are generally reproduced by all players
outside, on the benches. The point of this limit is to pressure the
players into actually developing a story rapidly and also resolving it
at a satisfying time. Note however that the hand signals are very easy
to miss, as they must be discreet enough not to bother the audience.\
\
This concludes our blitz summary of what the audience may see of an
improvised theater show, the varnish, but there is one final aspect, one
last meta-data the improvisers can use on stage, and that comes from
training. Players usually know each other and train together multiple
times a week. And players have habits, habits that you cannot help but
notice once you're familiar with them. For some, it is recurring themes
in the narratives they lead, for others, it is recurring characters they
incarnate. Once you know an improviser well enough, a lot can be
inferred about what they are about to play from the simple fact that
*they* are about to play it.\
\
To sum it all up, here follow the different means of communication
(which we will often call "actions\", to gamify) players have access to
while performing: theme, dialogue, acting, caucus, bench-planning, time
and habit. We order those in Table 1

|   Available to the audience |  to all improvisers |  to some improvisers |
|  ---------------------------| --------------------| ---------------------|
|             theme           |        caucus       |    bench-planning |
|           dialogue          |         time        |         habit |
|            acting           |                      | |

Table 1 : means of communication

[^2]. Constraints can be ignored for simplification, but they are
crucial in the sense they allow or forbid some of these canals[^3]. The
ultimate goal of this essay is both to formalise each of these aspects
into a relevant DEL framework, finding an appropriate system for us to
do so, and to question the metaphysics behind the statements
communicated themselves. So, first we must discuss the stance the
remaining pages will assume on the place of knowledge in fiction.

## Knowledge in fiction

"How can fictitious facts be known if knowing requires truth?\". One
could already discuss the nature of a "fictitious fact\", an apparent
oxymoron, but let us treat facts as simple statements for now. The first
thing to clarify is that, for the rest of the paper, our definition of
knowledge (and belief) will not exactly match that which we are used to.
Instead, it must be double. We separate cases where someone knows
something about the play itself and cases where someone knows something
about what is happening in the play. For example, we distinguish
statements like \"I know the play is in french\" from \"I know that Bob
is Alice's father\", given Bob and Alice two characters in the play.
More specifically, for the second one, we will assume systematic cheeky
ellipses, allowing for some rephrasing: "I know Bob is Alice's father\"
will be interpreted as "I know that, in the scene, Bob is Alice's
father\". In short, knowledge of the scene being played out will always
be relativised to the context of the scene itself. This allows us to
merge both understandings of knowledge into one, of which the object is
systematically the scene, and then either some of its properties, or
some "aboutness\" (the characters, their relationship, in general: what
the scene is depicting). Suppose instead that "I know Bob is Alice's
father\" was interpreted as is, then it would be one of those
"fictitious facts\", in the sense that it refers to some fictitious
non-entities. But how can a statement refer to something that is not?
Some researchers actually defend that "the use of a singular term is
said to have ontological commitment iff it has been chosen while
substituting a bounded variable that occurs in an existentially
quantified expression. Accordingly, in an argumentative context, a
proponent is not entitled to the thesis that, say, *Vampires exist*, by
the opponent's concession that *Nosferatu* is such a
creature\"[Rahman]. In a similar way, saying "Bob is Alice's father\"
does not necessarily entail that both characters actually exist. It only
talks about their relation, or properties.\
\
But this bizarre disjointedness between what is true of the scene and of
the outside world is far from the only quirk emerging from epistemically
treated fiction. Consider the aforementioned paradox of fiction.
Formulated by Radford and Weston in [Radford], it is constructed from
three observations, inconsistent when taken altogether yet seemingly
self-evident, which we reformulate as follows:

-   We have emotions for fictitious characters, despite knowing them not
    to be real.

-   We have emotions only for what we believe to be real.

-   We cannot know something to be fictitious yet believe it to be real.

Now, the goal of this section is far from trying to give an answer to
this paradox, very much still debated, with philosophers usually
claiming one of the statements is not as evident as it may seem.
Instead, simply notice the use of *believe* and *know* in the clauses:
this paradox fully relies on how we engage with the story, how we treat
the characters and scenes that it portrays. Now, going back to the
dichotomy we presented before, this type of belief here mentioned is not
"contained\" in the scene, it is not of the same kind as "I know Bob is
Alice's father\". Instead it appears to be that this knowledge
disregards the relativisation of the characters to their scene. Knowing
a character is not real is crucially different from knowing they are not
real in the scene. Although there are many ways to treat characters and
events of fiction (the Meinongian, possibilist, contextualist accounts,
etc.[Thomasson]), it still seems that, at the core, we treat them as
entities of their own, which can be extracted out of their scenes. This
hints at the similarity in treatment between fictitious characters and
actual entities, to which we will come back later on. Our
recontextualisation's main point is to handle factivity of knowledge,
bounding truth to the scene, even if characters were to "leak out\"
[^4].\
\
However, it is essential to notice that everything we have said so far
only holds for fiction with set in stone statements, for we have only
been talking about knowledge. You, as the author, know what will happen
next if, for instance, you have already written it. But when it comes to
the audience (and improvisers), there is but one way of engaging with
the material: belief, not knowledge. The audience does not know that a
certain character is nice, for said character might be traitor later on,
they only believe so. And similarly for improvisers. Although, one could
argue that in a one person improvised show, said improviser could *know*
what is going to happen, or whether said character is nice, as they are
in full control and might have already decided on it. However, most
improvisation is played in groups, and no one knows whether the others
are planning the same thing as them, nor whether they have understood
the story so far at all. As such, knowledge must be ruled out, for there
is undeniable uncertainty in improvised fiction. This crucially prevents
us from modeling improvisation with standard public announcements logic.
As, not only the facts themselves can be later on contested (as we will
see further down), but the players themselves cannot know whether the
announcement has truly reached everyone, and for we know that common
knowledge requires this infinite chain of "knowledge of someone else's
knowledge\", that also cannot be used in our model. There is
nevertheless one point at which knowledge is possible: the very end.
After an improvised show, the scene played out is set in stone. Both the
audience and the improvisers can retrospectively know what happened.
This will come back.

## Thoughts and communication

As established previously, we are actually working with belief rather
than knowledge, as knowledge only comes at the end of the play, when
everything has been settled and no changes can be made. As such, there
is one appropriate framework for us to use: belief revision. We will be
using the standard system of belief revision, with however two small
additions:

-   To account for multiple agents, and a fortiori to clearly show
    divergences in belief, we will structure the belief orderings into a
    grid, where each line is the belief order of a specific agent.
    Additionally, we also link together identical worlds across multiple
    agents' belief. This implies that inconsistencies in ordering are
    visually represented as crossing lines, and that "the wider the
    cross, the more different the agents' beliefs are\".

-   We also suppose, for clarity, that the possible worlds present in
    the belief orders are merely representative of an equivalence class,
    and only *considering* a new proposition can separate said classes
    into two, depending on what those imply for the truth of said
    proposition.

But what do we mean by *considering*? Well, anytime a proposition is the
object of one of our aforementioned means of communication, no matter
whether it has been correctly transferred or not. That and, crucially,
inner thoughts. Indeed, given the creative nature of improvised theater,
one key difference emerges with usual cases of belief revision: the
agent does not necessarily need a trusted source of information to
change their beliefs, but instead we can consider that, by actively
contributing to the play itself, the beliefs of the agent may later on
turn into truth. Indeed, "as a result of the Success postulate, the
incoming formula F that brings about the belief change is assumed to be
completely trustworthy: the agent accepts without question the incoming
information F and incorporates it into her set of beliefs as per the
belief change process.\"[^5][The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy]. As such, improvisers could be
seen as their own trusted source, strongly separating them from the
audience in how they engage with the material and the proposition. The
audience received, the players create. This change, simple in
appearance, is actually more akin to a paradigm shift[^6]: where truth
generally precedes belief, here we go the other way around. Despite
belief revision being usually perceived as \"corrective\", despite
generally assuming that it is when \"our beliefs are wrong, \[that\] we
engage in dynamic procedures of belief revision\"[van Benthem],
improvisation requires to treat belief revision as a creative tool in
itself. Now, it is still essential to consider what each agent knows of
the concerned mean of communication, or the thought, i.e. whether it is
discreet, obvious, an aside, a private joke, etc. indeed, \"in order to
understand and formalize belief revision, it is essential to take into
account the actual 'learning event' that 'triggered' the belief change.
For example, our beliefs about the current situation after hearing a
public announcement are different from our beliefs after receiving a
fully private announcement. \[\...\] In contrast, our beliefs about the
triggering action are irrelevant as far our static revision is
concerned: our conditional beliefs about the current situation given
some hypothetical information do not depend on the way this information
might be acquired\" [Baltag]. Also, note that, in the spirit of
standard DEL, we assume discrete intervals of time, with every action
spanning a whole unit[^7].

## Weak and strong assertions

Before properly describing each action, or "trigger\", in belief
revision terms, let us test the waters first and come back to one of our
key challenges to account for: that when some new piece of information
contradicts what the audience believes, they can have two main types of
reaction: acceptance or rejection. Suppose that in a scene, some actor
presents themselves as "Alice\" and that, later on, for the same
character, someone calls them "Bob\". The audience is most likely going
to notice the change and resist against it, assuming the second
improviser has made a mistake, as it contradicts what was previously
established. Now, on the other hand, suppose that a whole scene is
depicted with gravitas, a solemn tone, characters fearing they might
die, all to later realise, as the punchline, that they were simply going
to the dentist. Now, once again, this contradicts what the audience
believed, as they probably expected stakes matching with the tone.
Notice however that such a thing would not be considered a mistake, but
as we said a *punchline*. Breaking expectations and pre-established
facts is one of the key methods to constructing jokes. Improvisers
constantly walk a tight rope, with on one side the risk of breaking
immersion and, on the other, that of simply not being funny, or just
being too predictable. Our model should then account for these
differences.\
\
One easy way to treat rejection is to assign to our first example a
belief update, and to our second multiple radical belief upgrades.
Indeed, one can notice that the key difference between our two examples
is that, in one case, the new information contradicts a statement that
was clearly communicated (the name), and that in the other it simply
contradicts was was implied yet never confirmed (the tone). This system,
at first glance, seems perfectly efficient, allowing us to explain the
visceral rejection of the audience as a simple mismatch between the
worlds left after the upgrade (all the worlds where the character's name
is Alice) and the new statement (that it is Bob). Our second case of
pure implication, on the other hand, does not result in rejection from
the audience as it was never mentioned that the play was about anything
other than going to the dentist. Hence no contradiction.\
\
Another approach would be to claim that rejection does not only arise
from contradiction with the pre-established facts, but also with an
implied, pragmatical statement. For instance, that "No one has more than
one name\", and similar propositions or "rules\" that, in general, we
use and assume day to day. In that case, both types of assertion could
be understood as radical belief upgrades, with rejection emerging from
the impossibility for the audience of finding a best world satisfying
all propositions at once (both names and the meta-statement about names)
without being contradictory.\
\
Now, both methods seem to work on their own. However, there is an
argument to be made in favour of the second one. Consider our previous
example with one improviser renaming the character Alice to "Bob\".
Usually, players will solve this mistake by claiming that the character
played by the improviser who made mistake was in fact the one
misremembering. In short, we shift the mistake from the improviser to
the character, with someone saying something the likes of "You can't
even remember the names of my friends, how am I supposed to trust
you?\", simultaneously addressing the mistake and utilising it to add
depth to the scene, introducing new stakes. This could still works with
our first method were we to treat both the mistake and the players
addressing it as the cause of a singular update, but the other most
common way of treating such a mistake does not grant the same favour.
That other technique is to actually have the mistake be right. Suppose
that, later on, Alice comes out and says "I have been lying this whole
time, going under many aliases\". Then, because our initial statement
that "the character's name is Alice\" was taken, as we assumed, as a
belief update, there exists no world left satisfying the proposition
that "the character's name is not Alice\". As such, this new revelation
should also end in a contradiction, and therefore be rejected by the
audience as some kind of mistake. Yet it is not. It is, as we said,
taken as a *revelation*, in much the same ways as the dentist example
was, a plot-twist. This double nature of how people interpret
contradiction seems to be the final nail in the coffin for that
particular method. One could argue that instead of taking naming
statements as "the character's name is Alice\", we should take them as
"that character was called Alice\", weakening the proposition, and
allowing worlds where the wrong name was used in the beginning to still
hold true. This however prevents us from explaining the perceived
mistake, as "that character was called Alice\" and "that character was
called Bob\", are non-contradictory[^8].\
\
Both mending cases can however be explained by our second method.
Suppose the mistake is taken as wrong, and the blame shifted to the
character, then the contradiction arises for there was a presupposition
that "What characters say is right\", which gets canceled or at least
weakened by addressing the mistake. On the other hand, suppose that
Alice turned out to be lying, then that same statement (or the weaker
"Characters do not lie\"[^9]) is the one at the core of the
contradiction, canceled by the revelation. This, however, crucially
relies on the audience's own pragmatic inference, and therefore relies
on the characters being treated in much the same way we treat actual
people, it relies on the scenes being treated in the same way that we
treat real life situations. Once again, we come back to the paradox of
fiction, where we treat fictional beings and scenarios as if they were
true, despite knowing they are not[^10].\
\
A final point to raise, which we have not yet addressed, is the nature
of said pragmatic inferences. We assumed earlier that those were bound
to the scene. Saying "Alice does not lie\" is akin to saying that "In
the scene, the character of Alice does not lie\". We could also consider
these inferences to be about the scene itself, of the meta kind
mentioned above. In theory, that could be possible, but, and this
strictly comes from personal experience with fiction and arts in
general, it does not seem to be how people think of the content. We
assume values and properties of the characters. There still are
assumptions of the improvisers (for example that "the improvisers treat
traumatic subjects appropriately\"), assumptions outside the scene, but
those do not seem to produce the same kind of rejection. Forgetting a
name will get a laugh, breaking a social rule will make people wince.

## Upgrade and updates

Let us finally properly scrutinise each action, and determine whether
they should be treated as belief updates or radical belief revisions, as
well as their reach. We suppose a toy example, with two improvisers and
an audience, respectively named I1, I2 and A. Each action will be
considered in a vacuum, that is without any pre-established beliefs from
any agent[^11]. Therefore, we represent our starting situation as:

```tikzcd
\begin{tikzcd}
I1 & true \\
I2 & true \arrow[u, no head] \\
A  & true \arrow[u, no head]
\end{tikzcd}
```

### Dialogue

As mentioned in the previous section, it seems that dialogue, to allow
for retroactive changes, must be considered as a radical belief upgrade.
There might however be some exceptions to that treatment, mainly one
that comes to mind: fairytales. In fairytale-style improvisation scenes,
one of the improvisers plays the role of the narrator, describing and
setting up the scene. As it is traditionally taken to be an omniscient
narrator, it would appear logical to treat what is said as gospel, and
therefore view it under the light of an update. To account for both
interpretations, let us make a more precise distinction between two
types of dialogue: in and out-of character. The reason is simple: we
previously advocated for dialogues as radical belief upgrades for it was
possible to mend mistakes by shifting the blame from the improviser to
the character. However, if the sentence itself was not said
in-character, then this strategy is not possible anymore. Treating
out-of-character statements as updates therefore does not risk leading
to any contradiction. From there on, two philosophical stances can be
assumed: either we choose to treat every piece of information coming
from dialogue as a radical belief upgrade to avoid splitting cases, or
we choose to allow for some belief updates, as a way of simplifying the
models by removing worlds that would never be re-instantiated. Purely
arbitrarily, let us choose the latter. We must also note that, although
it is always possible for one of the agents to miss the
information[^12], dialogue is always targeted towards both the audience
and all other players. This leaves us with the two following cases,
assuming dialogue is clearly communicating $\varphi$.

-   In-character:

```tikzcd
\begin{tikzcd}
I1 & \varphi & \neg\varphi \arrow[l, Rightarrow] \arrow[d, no head] \\
I2 & \varphi \arrow[u, no head] & \neg\varphi \arrow[l, Rightarrow] \arrow[d, no head] \\
A  & \varphi \arrow[u, no head] & \neg\varphi \arrow[l, Rightarrow]
\end{tikzcd}
```

-   Out-of-character:

```tikzcd
\begin{tikzcd}
I1 & \varphi \\
I2 & \varphi \arrow[u, no head] \\
A  & \varphi \arrow[u, no head]
\end{tikzcd}
```

Interestingly, note that knowing which improviser said $\varphi$ is
irrelevant for our system. Indeed, it is only useful when treating cases
where one of the agents did not understand what was communicated.
Indeed, suppose that I1 said it, then there would be no model where I1
is mistaken[^13].

### Acting

Acting is even easier to treat than dialogue. By acting we mean facial
expressions, gestures, voices, occupation of the space, etc. All these
can absolutely communicate information (think of, for instance, an actor
crying, which would usually communicate that their character is sad),
but, given that the parallel between acting and propositions is even
more obscure than with dialogue itself, there is no guarantee for anyone
that they have understood the actual intent of the actor. As such, only
radical belief upgrades can be considered. Acting is also, very much
like dialogue, directed towards both the audience and all improvisers,
making it another symmetrical revision. It can therefore be modeled in
much the same way as in-character dialogue, that being:

```tikzcd
\begin{tikzcd}
I1 & \varphi & \neg\varphi \arrow[l, Rightarrow] \arrow[d, no head] \\
I2 & \varphi \arrow[u, no head] & \neg\varphi \arrow[l, Rightarrow] \arrow[d, no head] \\
A  & \varphi \arrow[u, no head] & \neg\varphi \arrow[l, Rightarrow]            
\end{tikzcd}
```

### Theme

Let us consider the formula statement $\varphi$ as "the scene is about
\[the theme\]\"[^14], whatever the announced theme may be. As mentioned
earlier, respecting the theme is one of the mandatory constraints to all
improvisers, and the audience knows it. Therefore, there is no
possibility for the scene to play out in a way that contradicts the
theme. It can thus be safely considered a belief update. As it is
clearly announced and known of everyone, we treat it similarly to
out-of-character dialogue, that being:

```tikzcd
\begin{tikzcd}
I1 & \varphi \\
I2 & \varphi \arrow[u, no head] \\
A  & \varphi \arrow[u, no head]
\end{tikzcd}
```

### Caucus

Caucus is our first example of asymmetrical revision, that is that there
will be a difference between the belief order of improvisers and that of
the audience. Indeed, caucus is specifically intended for the audience
to miss, as it would otherwise spoil what may happen in the scene. But
when it comes down to which revision we should choose, we are presented
with a dilemma: on one hand, caucus is the closest thing in
improvisation to a set in stone truth, it is the intent of bringing the
story in a certain direction, of which everyone is aware. But on the
other, it is still possible for an improviser to go off the rails,
especially if they notice during the scene that there is a much more
interesting story available if they stray for the supposed path. The
first would encourage us to see it as a belief update, the second as an
upgrade. Despite the fact that caucus is in general overwhelmingly
respected, the slight chance of deviation seems to tilt the scales in
favour of radical belief upgrades. Were we to use a probabilistic model,
we could model the difference between belief in the caucus and belief in
dialogue as of different magnitude, but our current system cannot
account for this. Therefore, we model caucus of $\varphi$ as:

```tikzcd
\begin{tikzcd}
I1 & \varphi & \neg\varphi \arrow[l, Rightarrow] \arrow[d, no head]   \\
I2 & \varphi \arrow[u, no head] & \neg\varphi \arrow[l, Rightarrow] \arrow[d, no head]   \\
A  & \varphi \arrow[u, no head] & \neg\varphi \arrow[l, no head]
\end{tikzcd}
```

### Bench-planning

In that regard, bench planning is very similar to caucus, with one
simple difference: not all improvisers are involved. Suppose for this
example that we add an extra improviser I3 to the mix, the only one not
involved in the planning. Then, because I3 could go against what I1 and
I2 discuss, it is not possible to treat planning of $\varphi$ as a
belief update. Note that this does not apply to the caucus case despite
it also having an agent not involved in the discussion (the audience)
because the audience cannot interact with the play. I3, however, can.
Therefore, we must treat bench-planning similarly to caucus, with a
subset of all improvisers being concerned. In our expanded example, this
would result in:

```tikzcd
\begin{tikzcd}
I1 & \varphi & \neg\varphi \arrow[l, Rightarrow] \arrow[d, no head] \\
I2 & \varphi \arrow[u, no head] & \neg\varphi \arrow[l, Rightarrow] \arrow[d, no head] \\
I3 & \varphi \arrow[u, no head] & \neg\varphi \arrow[l, no head] \\
A  & \varphi \arrow[u, no head] \arrow[r, no head] & \neg\varphi \arrow[u, no head]
\end{tikzcd}
```

### Thoughts

We treat inner thought as an even more restricted case of
bench-planning, one which involves only one improviser. Note that for
the same reasons as previously it must be considered a radical belief
upgrade, and not an update. One small exception here: we can also
consider thought of the audience. This could allow us to treat cases of
suggestion, where nothing is explicitly communicated but the audience
themselves come to a conclusion. This however escapes the scope of this
particular essay. Let us suppose that I1 is the one having a thought,
then we model it as such:

```tikzcd
\begin{tikzcd}
I1 & \varphi & \neg\varphi \arrow[l, Rightarrow] \arrow[d, no head] \\
I2 & \varphi \arrow[u, no head] & \neg\varphi \arrow[d, no head] \arrow[l, no head] \\
A  & \varphi \arrow[u, no head] & \neg\varphi \arrow[l, no head]
\end{tikzcd}
```

### Habit

For habit is an assumption from some improvisers, we treat it
identically to bench planning. There is a philosophical difference
however, namely that bench planning is a collective effort, whereas
noticing habits is an individual thought happening in multiple people.
However, this is irrelevant to our modeling, similarly to how the
precise speaker was irrelevant to the dialogue case (for only the result
here matters[^15]), and, supposing I1 knows I2 well and predicts their
habits of doing $\varphi$, we represent the situation as:

```tikzcd
\begin{tikzcd}
I1 & \varphi & \neg\varphi \arrow[l, Rightarrow] \arrow[d, no head] \\
I2 & \varphi \arrow[u, no head] & \neg\varphi \arrow[d, no head] \arrow[l, no head] \\
A  & \varphi \arrow[u, no head] & \neg\varphi \arrow[l, no head]
\end{tikzcd}
```

### Time

As mentioned earlier, time indications are extremely discreet as not to
perturb the audience. This means that they can very easily be missed by
the improvisers too, especially if they happen to not be facing the
referee. We consider the statement $\varphi$ as "the scene is about to
end\" which, once again, as it is a requirement players must abide to,
cannot be contested and thus can be interpreted as a belief update to
those who saw it. Similarly to how bench planning was an upgrade to a
subset of the improvisers, time is instead an update to a subset of the
improvisers. Supposing only I1 sees the signs, we model it as:

```tikzcd
\begin{tikzcd}
I1 & \varphi & \\
I2 & \varphi \arrow[u, no head] & \neg\varphi \arrow[d] \arrow[l] \\
A  & \varphi \arrow[u, no head] & \neg\varphi \arrow[l]
\end{tikzcd}
```

### Ending

There is one final action that we have not yet mentioned, or at least
not claimed we would properly look at, and that is the announcement of
the end of the play. When the play ends, as we have said previously,
there is no possibility of change, no rectification allowed. We treat
endings as private updates to every agent, where $\varphi$ is the
formula singling out the best worlds of each belief order. There is no
need for $\varphi$ to be the same for all agents, as everyone might have
a different perception (especially if improvisers had planned things
that where never communicated, then their best world would not
necessarily be the best world of the audience, and there is no reason to
believe that what the improvisers believed yet never said would
influence what the audience takes away from the play).

## Framework limitations and possible expansions

Now, it is important to notice that the system here presented has some
very obvious caveats. Other than the ones mentioned in footnotes and the
main text itself, here follow a few:

-   First of all, the structure and wording of both communicated
    propositions and pragmatic inferences are still very much hazy. We
    mentioned the possibility to account for naming as "This character's
    name is Alice\" or "This character was called Alice\", but actually
    there seems to be a semi-infinite amount of relevant information.
    For instance, maybe we should word it as "This character was called
    Alice and they answered\", seemingly strengthening the assumption
    that it indeed is their name. We previously assumed equivalence
    classes of worlds, where only the relevant proposition are actually
    considered. But what is the relevant proposition? Should the
    relevant proposition contain every single bit of information that
    could later on be relevant, at the cost of being very clearly too
    heavy, and losing parallels with the human mind which does not
    appear to think in gargantuously long propositions, or instead
    should we only consider the smallest proposition currently relevant,
    as in that no other rewordings would add any information
    contradicting or supporting what is already established, at the cost
    of having to retrospectively change it when new information is added
    that could interact with a different, previously ignored facet of
    what was said before? There is a high level of arbitrariness.

-   We could also consider the issue of tone, and general suggestion. In
    our dentist example, the general tone was the setup to the
    punchline, but how do we characterise tone in logical formulas? At
    the very least it would require higher order logic, in a similar way
    to semantical interpretations of adverbs, which are probably the
    closest linguistic object we have to tone. The same issue can be
    said of acting, and in general translating non-linguistic means into
    proper propositions. Nevertheless this seems even more arbitrary
    than standard propositions, also given how different people can
    perceive a same situation under very different lights.

-   Another limitation is the set amount of people on stage. In our toy
    examples, we only assumed two (or three) improvisers and one
    audience for simplicity. Of course, we could have gone for more but
    more importantly the number of involved agents never changed.
    However, as was mentioned before, improvisers have the opportunity
    to exit and enter the stage at any point. This can be treated in two
    ways, either by considering all improvisers as agents, regardless of
    whether they are on stage or not, or we could use a system allowing
    for changing agents, in a similar way as [Singh]. Now, in
    general both options work, but changing agents could be used to also
    handle heckling, the practice from one member of the audience to
    intervene in the show unprompted, by yelling or thankfully more
    rarely coming on stage. This seems like a proper case where an agent
    is added, as having the heckler considered an agent from the get-go
    appears, as a philosophical concern, to have them be a heckler
    before anything is done. The same thing can be said for shows with
    audience participation, whether that is suggesting themes, or, this
    time desirably, coming on stage.

## Conclusion

type of revision, as seen in Table 2, in which we can observe a general tendency for updates to reach as far
as possible, and for upgrades to be more lenient with smaller groups.

Overall, the study of creation, and a fortiori improvisation, is very
new to being studied under the epistemic light, as seems to indicate the
extremely narrow scope of relevant papers. It feels however promising,
appearing as an interesting entry way into the study of potential
factive beliefs and fiction pragmatics. One can only hope that, one day,
if deemed worthy enough, it will join other fields of study on the main
*stage*.
