## Introduction

The goal of this essay is to explore possible applications of the DEL
framework to one specific case: that of improvised theater. We know that
the relationship between knowledge and fiction has always been arduous,
or at the very least complicated ; the paradox of fiction being a great
example of their everlasting conflict. How can fictitious facts be known
if knowing requires truth? With only two ingredients already emerge
incredibly intricate questions. However, I personally believe that
improvisation, when added to the mix, results in even grander wonders.
Indeed, it implies one major key difference: the text has not yet been
written, and as such "truth\", even in its weakest definitions, appears
frail, unstable, undetermined. There is a fundamental lack of, well,
fundamentals, no baseline guiding the performance from start to end.
Improvisers decide what the story will be as they go. And although they
obviously must communicate it to the audience to make a compelling show,
thus avoiding entre-soi, they must also, even more crucially,
communicate it amongst themselves. This, more often than not, results in
misunderstandings and multiple interpretations of a same scene,
misunderstandings that the audience loves, as a peak behind the curtain
of a performer's mind, because the audience assumes what should have
happened. They "know\" the performer misremembered a character's name.
They "know\" that what has been set up will come back later in the
scene. And the improvisers "know\" where they want to take it.\
\
Could then the logical definition of knowledge, or at least certain
belief, be applied to such cases? What is the difference in treatment
between truths from the point of view of the actors and that of the
audience? Why are there statements that are easy to retract or bend for
great entertainment and some that, when reevaluated, make you wonder if
the people on stage are actually listening to one another? I will
attempt to give an answer, albeit a very partial one, to those
questions, using my knowledge and experience of improvised theater, both
on and off stage.

## A brief overview of improvised theater

\</p\>\<p class=\"mb-3\"\>Now, obviously, it would be hard to follow
along without any idea of what improvised theater is. I will be
explaining the style which I am most familiar with: the style most
common to French-speaking Switzerland, particularly Geneva [@fig],
though it has similarities with that of Amsterdam.\
\
Improvised theater is, as anyone could guess, theater that is
improvised. In Geneva, it is played as a match, though this is of little
importance to us here. Most of the time, the scenes[^1] are quite short
and have to respect both a constraint of form and a theme. The
constraint can be any limitation or style imposed on the players (for
instance that all lines must rhyme, or that the scene must be played
following Western-movie codes). The theme on the other hand is generally
a single word or sentence to kickstart dialogue and plot-lines. Respect
of both is mandatory, under threat of a penalty for the unabiding team.
This necessarily means there is some meta aspect to improvisations; that
there are some elements that come from outside the dialogue and acting.
We will come back to this a few pages down.\
\
If you were to attend a show, you would notice that before a scene is
played out, both the constraint and its theme are announced by a
referee, then the players get a few seconds (called the "caucus\") to
argue for a general story, or to decide who plays which character.
Therefore, there is a small window of time to decide of what is, to
bastardise the term, common knowledge to all the players. However, not
all scenes allow for caucuses, as some constraints explicitly forbid
it.\
\
During improvisation, it is allowed for the actors not yet performing to
enter the stage at any time, and for any actor to leave and return to
the resting benches (again, if the constraint allows for it, as some
like "huis-clos\" explicitly anchor actors on stage). Thus, it is
possible for actors to scheme and discuss amongst benchwarmers before
they join in the scene. It is, however, not allowed for anyone outside
the scene to address anyone inside: once a player is on stage, all
exchange of information with them must go through acting, and acting
alone.\
\
Furthermore, all scenes are played under a time limit, imposed with the
constraint. Time is regularly communicated through hand signals given by
the referee, signals which are generally reproduced by all players
outside, on the benches. The point of this limit is to pressure the
players into actually developing a story rapidly and also resolving it
at a satisfying time. Note however that the hand signals are very easy
to miss, as they must be discreet enough not to bother the audience.\
\
This concludes our blitz summary of what the audience may see of an
improvised theater show, the varnish, but there is one final aspect, one
last meta-data the improvisers can use on stage, and that comes from
training. Players usually know each other and train together multiple
times a week. And players have habits, habits that you cannot help but
notice once you're familiar with them. For some, it is recurring themes
in the narratives they lead, for others, it is recurring characters they
incarnate. Once you know an improviser well enough, a lot can be
inferred about what they are about to play from the simple fact that
\<em class=\"italic\"\>they\</em\> are about to play it.\
\
To sum it all up, here follow the different means of communication
(which we will often call "actions\", to gamify) players have access to
while performing: theme, dialogue, acting, caucus, bench-planning, time
and habit. We order those in Table 1

   Available to the audience  | to all improvisers |  to some improvisers
  ------------------------------------------------------ |-------------------- |---------------------
                          theme                          |       caucus        |   bench-planning
                         dialogue                        |        time         |        habit
                          acting                         |                     |

ultimate goal of this essay is both to formalise each of these aspects
into a relevant DEL framework, finding an appropriate system for us to
do so, and to question the metaphysics behind the statements
communicated themselves. So, first we must discuss the stance the
remaining pages will assume on the place of knowledge in fiction.\
\</p\>

[^1]: We will be using the words "scene\" and "play\" interchangeably.

[^2]: We here assume for structural purposes that time indications are
